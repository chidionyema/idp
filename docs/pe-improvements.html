Why the schedules miss

Seven of the ten daily jobs on this estate have never run. Not once. They are not late and they are not broken — they are booked for hours when the laptop is asleep. Here is the class of problem, what changes, and what is still yours to decide.

The class, stated once

Every time this comes up it gets treated as a broken job, and one job gets repaired. That is why it keeps coming back. The actual class is work placed on hardware that cannot honour it, watched by instruments nobody reads, reported under a word that means two different things.

Three separate failures wearing one name. A MacBook sleeps: a job booked for 03:30 does not run at 03:30, and no amount of fixing the script changes that. A dead-man check that has never received a ping looks identical on a board to one that pinged an hour ago. And a job that ran and exited non-zero is filed under the same phrase as a job that never started.

What to expect

Six improvements. Three are shipped and provable tonight, two are next, and one is a decision only you can make. Each carries the check that would show it failed.

1. Placement becomes a rule instead of a diagnosis

Shipped

PE capability · Golden paths — a paved road with guardrails (LAW 38)

Was
Nobody could say which of 43 jobs belonged on the laptop and which needed a machine that stays awake. Each missed schedule was triaged on its own.
Now
A job's host is a policy decision, graded by command. Three hard rules: a schedule inside the sleep window, a monitored job that has never pinged, and survival work — backup, restore drill, key escrow — whose only schedule is on the machine it protects.
Expect
The recurring ticket stops. The baseline to beat is 7 of 10 daily jobs dead; a run that reports zero unexplained placements is the finish line.
Check
bin/placement-audit — currently 22 failures, 12 warnings across 43 jobs.

2. Guards have to prove they say yes, not just no

Shipped

PE capability · Self-service with guardrails (LAW 38)

Was
Guards were tested on the case they refuse. Two consequences, both real this week: the documentation gate refused a push that carried both required files under their real name, twice; and the licence policy passed a scan that found 2 licences in 1,821 packages, printing "licences clean" having checked nothing.
Now
Every policy ships paired controls — a fixture that must pass beside a fixture that must fail — and the runner checks exit codes rather than reading output. The licence policy also now refuses a scan where under 10% of packages carry terms, because a guard that passes on empty input is worse than no guard.
Expect
A guard that starts refusing correct work shows up in a test run instead of in a blocked push at midnight. A guard that starts passing everything shows up at all.
Check
bin/policy-test — 5 fixtures, 2 policies, all behaving as specified.

The guard that got quieter

An hour after this page was published, the audit was re-run and reported 11 failures where it had reported 22. Nothing on the estate had improved. The guard had gone blind and stopped talking.

What happened: the Healthchecks container went unhealthy — its request workers were being killed for exceeding their 30-second limit while the machine sat at load average 7.71. bin/placement-audit could no longer read any check record, so it wrote "check": null against all 34 monitored jobs. The rule that catches a job which has never pinged reads job.check.last_ping == null, and in Rego null.last_ping is undefined rather than null — so the rule body simply failed and the rule stopped matching. Eleven real failures disappeared from the report.

This is the worst failure shape a guard has, because the output looks like progress. A guard that goes silent when it loses its evidence will be believed exactly when it should not be.

Fixed: a monitored job whose check cannot be read is now refused outright, with its own message saying the state is unknown rather than fine. Unknown is not pass. And the audit reports BLIND separately from never-pinged, because adding a monitoring outage into a job-failure count makes each look like the other.

Proven by a fixture that isolates it — one job, breaking that rule and no other, so no other rule can mask a regression:

$ bin/policy-test
placement-blind.json  1  1  refuses a monitored job whose check could not be
                            read, so a monitoring outage cannot read as a pass

PASS      every policy allows its good case and refuses its bad ones

This is improvement 2 doing its job on improvement 1, which is the only reason it was caught within the hour rather than at the next incident.

Corrected again, and this one was mine twice over

This page said there were two inventories on the estate that do not join, and cited the estate's own silos=2 finding as agreement. Both halves were wrong, and I found it by reading bin/db-gen instead of trusting my own note.

There is one inventory. bin/catalog-gen and bin/db-gen are both adapters over it — one writes Backstage YAML, the other writes SQLite for Datasette. Joined on id, every one of the 204 inventory rows is present in the catalogue. The catalogue holds one extra, zz.probe.badxml, because its snapshot was taken 44 seconds earlier. Two counts that differ are not two silos.

And silos=2 counts two findings that have nothing to do with the catalogue: work recorded in 77 ledgers that do not join, and scheduled machinery running from 6 roots. I cited a real number against the wrong subject, which is the more dangerous of the two errors because the number checks out.

Onboarded: not fully, and here is the actual gap. Four features carry the demo and onboarding pair the rules require — the IDP itself, supply chain, placement, and Langfuse. The catalogue's 194 contents do not. Onboarding covers the platform's own features, not yet the assets inside it. There is also one fact with two implementations: catalog-gen and db-gen each compute whether a repo has a licence file by checking the same five filenames, because the inventory does not record it. They agree today only because both were written on the same day.

The architecture is drawn, verified and in the repo at docs/architecture.md — one source, two adapters, two swappable renderers, and where each guard sits in the flow. It did not exist until today, which is its own finding: the platform had six proven improvements and no picture of itself.

What this does not fix

The audit reads and grades. It does not move a single job, because moving one has a blast radius and that is not a script's decision. No always-on host exists yet, so the ten jobs that need one are still where they were an hour ago — the difference is that they are now on a list with a reason rather than surfacing one at a time as mystery failures.

The ten checks that ran and failed are entirely untouched by any of this. They are a separate list and they need reading one at a time.

Method. Everything on this page was measured between 02:00 and 03:00 on 24 August 2026 against the running system — the Healthchecks API, launchctl list, pmset -g log and the launchd property lists. Where a number could be got two ways it was: the ran-and-failed count agrees between Healthchecks and launchd's own exit statuses.

Weaker evidence. The sleep-window rule uses midnight to 08:00, which is a judgement about when this laptop is realistically shut rather than a measured constant. It is set in one place, policy/placement.rego, and every job it currently catches is also caught by the never-pinged rule — so no job is condemned by that judgement alone.
